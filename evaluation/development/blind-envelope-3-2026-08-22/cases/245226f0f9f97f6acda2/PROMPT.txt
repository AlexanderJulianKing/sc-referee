Author one complete, self-contained small analysis project for the scenario below. Deliver three
things in a single project folder: one data file in comma-separated form, one analysis script named
analysis.py sitting at the root of the project, and one short written report.

Scenario. A preclinical oncology experiment in rats. Twenty-four tumour-bearing animals were studied:
twelve given a candidate compound and twelve given the vehicle alone. Each animal's tumour was
measured by calliper once a week for five consecutive weeks, so each animal contributes five
measurements taken at five different times.

Data. Build the comma-separated file yourself with realistic invented values. Tumour volume should be
in cubic millimetres, starting near 180 in both groups and growing over the five weeks to about 880 in
the vehicle group and about 620 in the treated group. Animals should differ substantially from one
another, roughly 120 cubic millimetres of spread between animals by the final week, with about 60 of
measurement-to-measurement noise within an animal. Include the animal identifier, the treatment group,
the week number, the tumour volume, the animal's body weight in grams around 280, and the cage the
animal was housed in. Name the identifier column after the animal. Write column headers in lowercase
words joined by underscores.

Analysis. The primary inference must respect the fact that the five measurements from one animal are
not independent of each other. Fit a model of tumour volume on treatment group and week that includes
a per-animal random effect, and take the treatment estimate and its p-value from that model as the
study's result, reporting the sample size as twenty-four animals contributing one hundred and twenty
measurements. Separately, and clearly marked as a secondary sensitivity check rather than the
inferential result, also run a plain two-sample comparison of the final-week values treating rows as
independent, and print both outputs distinctly.

Report. Write a short report covering the animal experiment, the primary dependence-aware method and
why the repeated measurements require it, the primary result with its estimate and p-value, and then
the secondary check presented plainly as a supporting sensitivity analysis that is not the study's
inferential conclusion. Open the report with a short data description listing every column and stating
what one row represents.

Write it as a careful preclinical statistician would, in plain competent style.
