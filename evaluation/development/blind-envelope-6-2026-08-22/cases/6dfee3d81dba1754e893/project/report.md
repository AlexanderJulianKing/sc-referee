# Six-minute walk distance three months after total knee replacement

Comparison of standard supervised outpatient physiotherapy against a home-based programme with
weekly telephone coaching.

## Aim

To find out whether the distance a patient can walk in six minutes, measured three months after
total knee replacement, differs between two rehabilitation protocols: standard supervised
outpatient physiotherapy, and a home-based programme with weekly telephone coaching.

## Design

Forty-four patients were enrolled in an orthopaedic physiotherapy service and randomised to one of
the two protocols, twenty-two patients per protocol. At the three-month follow-up each patient
performed the six-minute walk test **once**, and that single distance is the study outcome.

Because the test was done once per patient, **each patient contributes exactly one measurement**.
The patient is therefore both the unit of randomisation, the thing that was assigned to a
protocol, and the unit of measurement, the thing that produced a number. The two are the same
here, and the data file has exactly one row per patient with no repeated patient identifiers. The
two protocol groups contain different patients, and no patient appears in both.

That design fact decides the statistics. The sample size for each protocol is 22 patients, which
is also 22 rows, so counting rows and counting patients give the same answer and there is no risk
of treating repeated measurements from one person as though they came from separate people.

## Data description

The project has one data file, `knee_rehab_6mwt.csv`, generated by `make_data.py` with a fixed
random seed so it reproduces exactly. It holds 44 data rows plus a header line.

**What one row represents.** One row is one patient, measured once at the three-month follow-up.
Every `patient_ref` value appears exactly once. Row count and patient count are the same number.

| Column | Type | Units | What it holds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `patient_ref` | text | none | Study identifier for the patient, `PT-001` through `PT-044`. Unique in the file: one code, one patient, one row. |
| `rehab_protocol` | text, two possible labels | none | The protocol the patient was randomised to. Either `standard_outpatient` (standard supervised outpatient physiotherapy) or `home_based_telephone` (home-based programme with weekly telephone coaching). 22 patients each. |
| `age_years` | number | years | Patient age at enrolment, one decimal place. Observed range 58.0 to 81.7 years. |
| `bmi` | number | kg/m^2 | Patient body mass index at enrolment, one decimal place. Observed range 22.3 to 37.9. |
| `six_min_walk_m` | number | metres | Distance walked in the six-minute walk test at the three-month follow-up, one decimal place. This is the outcome, and each patient contributes exactly one such value. Observed range 296.5 to 577.6 m. |

There are no missing outcome values.

## Method

`analysis.py` at the project root does all of the work. It reads the CSV, then in order:

1. **Checks the design before testing anything.** It counts rows and distinct `patient_ref`
   values and stops with an error if they differ, checks that no patient sits under both
   protocols, and checks for missing outcome values. The two-sample test is only valid when each
   patient contributes one measurement, so that property is verified in the file rather than
   assumed.
2. **Summarises both protocols**: number of patients, and the mean, standard deviation, median
   and range of six-minute walk distance, plus mean age and mean BMI.
3. **Compares the two protocols** with an independent two-sample t-test on the patient rows. This
   is the right test because the two groups hold different patients and each patient appears once,
   so the observations being compared are independent of one another. Welch's version is used as
   the primary test, which means it does not assume the two protocols have the same spread of
   distances. The reported sample size is the number of patients per protocol, which equals the
   number of rows per protocol.

Alongside the primary test the script reports three supporting checks: the Student t-test that
assumes equal variances, the Mann-Whitney U test, which compares ranks instead of means and so
does not assume a normal distribution, and Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests of the normality and
equal-variance assumptions. These are reported for transparency, not to choose the test after
seeing the data. The primary test was fixed in advance.

Software: Python 3, pandas 2.0.3, SciPy 1.9.1.

## Results

Design check, printed by the script: 44 rows, 44 distinct patients, 0 patients appearing more than
once, 0 patients under both protocols, 0 missing outcome values.

### Summary by protocol

| | Standard outpatient | Home-based, telephone coaching |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Patients (= rows) | 22 | 22 |
| Six-minute walk, mean | 404.0 m | 446.0 m |
| Six-minute walk, SD | 55.9 m | 69.8 m |
| Six-minute walk, median | 407.9 m | 442.3 m |
| Six-minute walk, range | 296.5 to 495.1 m | 306.1 to 577.6 m |
| Age, mean (SD) | 70.1 y (8.3) | 70.4 y (6.6) |
| BMI, mean (SD) | 29.6 (5.1) | 28.7 (4.8) |

The two groups are similar in age and BMI, so the comparison is not obviously distorted by one
protocol having had older or heavier patients.

### Primary comparison

Independent two-sample t-test (Welch), unit of analysis the patient, 22 patients per protocol:

- Difference, home-based minus standard outpatient: **+42.0 m**
- 95% confidence interval for the difference: **3.5 m to 80.5 m**
- Standard error: 19.06 m
- t = 2.203, df = 40.08, **p = 0.0334**
- Hedges' g = 0.652 (Cohen's d = 0.664), a moderate standardised effect

The confidence interval is the range of true differences that fit the data reasonably well. It
runs from 3.5 m to 80.5 m and does not include zero, which is the same information the p-value
carries.

### Supporting checks

| Check | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Student t-test, equal variance assumed | t = 2.203, p = 0.0332 |
| Mann-Whitney U, rank based | U = 327.5, p = 0.0460 |
| Shapiro-Wilk, standard outpatient arm | W = 0.969, p = 0.6874 |
| Shapiro-Wilk, home-based arm | W = 0.980, p = 0.9103 |
| Levene test of equal variances | W = 0.870, p = 0.3564 |

Distances look normally distributed inside each arm and the spreads are not distinguishable, so
the t-test assumptions hold up. The rank-based test agrees with the t-test, which means the result
does not depend on the choice of test.

## Clinical conclusion

At the three-month follow-up, patients in the home-based programme with weekly telephone coaching
walked on average 42.0 m further in six minutes than patients in standard supervised outpatient
physiotherapy (446.0 m against 404.0 m). The difference is statistically significant at the 5%
level (p = 0.0334, 95% CI 3.5 to 80.5 m).

Forty-two metres is a clinically meaningful amount for this population. Published estimates of the
smallest change patients notice on the six-minute walk test after knee replacement sit in the
region of 25 to 60 m, so the point estimate here is within the range that patients would feel in
daily walking. That said, the confidence interval is wide: the data are also compatible with a
gain as small as about 3.5 m, which no patient would notice, and with a gain as large as about
80 m. With 22 patients per protocol the study can show that a difference exists but cannot pin
down its size closely.

The practical reading is that the home-based programme with weekly telephone coaching is at least
as good as standard supervised outpatient physiotherapy on this outcome, and probably better. Since
home-based rehabilitation also removes travel and clinic attendance for the patient, this supports
offering it as a routine option. A larger study would be needed before claiming a specific size of
benefit.

### Limits of what this analysis shows

- The comparison covers one outcome at one time point, three months. It says nothing about pain,
  knee range of motion, patient satisfaction, complication rates, or anything beyond three months.
- Each patient walked once, so within-patient variability in walking performance from day to day
  is not measured, and it is folded into the between-patient spread.
- The analysis is unadjusted. Age and BMI both affect walking distance and both were recorded, but
  the protocol comparison here does not adjust for them. The two groups happened to be well
  matched on age and BMI, so adjustment would be unlikely to change the direction of the result,
  though it could shift the estimate somewhat.
- The single p-value comes from one prespecified primary test, so no correction for multiple
  comparisons is needed.
