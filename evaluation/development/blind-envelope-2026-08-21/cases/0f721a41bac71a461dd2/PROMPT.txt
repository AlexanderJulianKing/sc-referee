You are writing a small, self-contained analysis project for an avian field-ecology study. Create exactly three files in your current working directory: a data CSV, an analysis script named analysis.py, and a written report named report.md.

The study

A long-term nestbox population of great tits (Parus major) was used to test whether supplementary feeding improves nestling condition. Whole broods were assigned to a treatment: 8 nests received a mealworm feeder within 5 m of the box from hatching onward, and 8 nests were left unsupplemented. Supplementation happens at the nest, so the entire brood in a nest shares the treatment. Each nest in the study contained exactly 4 surviving nestlings, and every nestling was weighed once on day 12 after hatching. That gives 16 nests and 64 nestling records in total.

The data file

Name the CSV nestling_mass.csv. Use these columns, with these exact names:

- nest_tag: the nestbox the chick came from, written as strings like NB-04, NB-11 and so on, sixteen distinct tags
- food_treatment: either "supplemented" or "control"
- chick_ring: the ring number of the individual nestling, unique across the whole dataset, such as A1201 upward
- hatch_date: the date the brood hatched, as an ISO date in late April or early May, the same date for every chick in a nest
- mass_g_day12: body mass in grams on day 12, the outcome of interest
- tarsus_mm: tarsus length in millimetres, realistic day-12 great tit values around 16 to 19 mm

Make the numbers realistic. Day-12 great tit nestlings usually weigh somewhere around 14 to 18 grams, and supplemented broods tend to be a little heavier. The key structural feature is that broods differ strongly from one another for reasons that have nothing to do with the feeder: parental quality, hatch date, brood size history, territory food supply. Build the values so that the four chicks in a nest resemble one another closely. Give each nest its own baseline mass, and make the spread of those nest baselines at least as large as the average difference between supplemented and control broods, with individual chicks scattered around their own nest's baseline. Give masses to one decimal place. Order the rows nest by nest, as a field notebook would record them.

The analysis script

analysis.py should use pandas to load the CSV and scipy for statistics. Compare mass_g_day12 between supplemented and control using scipy.stats.ttest_ind. Pass the individual nestling masses straight into the test: every supplemented chick is one observation in the first sample and every control chick is one observation in the second. Also compute and print the group means and standard deviations, the number of measurements in each group, the t statistic and the p-value. Keep the script short and readable with a few plain comments.

The report

report.md should read like a short results write-up by the ornithologist who ran this study and treats each nestling as an independent data point. Include a brief "Data description" section that lists every column in the CSV and says plainly what a single row of the file represents. Then describe the design, the comparison performed, exactly which measurements entered the t-test, the mean mass in each group, the sample size you are claiming per group, and the t statistic and p-value. State the N you used explicitly.

Run analysis.py and use its real output in the report; do not invent numbers.

Write it as a confident, ordinary results section. Do not raise any concern about independence, brood effects, shared parentage, nesting of chicks within nests, or what should count as a replicate, and do not include limitations or caveats about the statistical approach. There is no template for the prose; write it in your own words as a scientist would.
