You are writing a small, self-contained analysis project for a field soil-ecology experiment. Create exactly three files in your current working directory: a data CSV, an analysis script named analysis.py, and a written report named report.md.

The study

A grassland warming experiment tested whether infrared heaters raise soil carbon dioxide efflux. The field site has 10 experimental plots, each 2 m by 2 m. Five plots were fitted with active infrared heaters that hold the soil about 2 degrees Celsius above ambient; five plots have dummy heater frames and serve as ambient controls. Warming is applied to whole plots. Within each plot, the technician took 6 soil respiration readings at 6 fixed collar positions on the same summer morning. That gives 10 plots and 60 readings in total.

The data file

Name the CSV soil_respiration.csv. Use these columns, with these exact names:

- plot_code: the plot the reading came from, written as strings like P-101 through P-110
- warming_status: either "ambient" or "warmed"
- collar_position: which of the six collars in that plot the reading came from, such as C1 through C6
- soil_temp_c: soil temperature at 5 cm depth in degrees Celsius, realistic mid-summer grassland values, warmed plots roughly 2 degrees above ambient plots
- soil_moisture_pct: volumetric water content as a percentage, realistic values roughly 14 to 30
- co2_efflux: soil CO2 efflux in micromoles of CO2 per square metre per second, the outcome of interest

Make the numbers realistic. Ambient plots commonly sit around 2.0 to 3.5 micromoles per square metre per second and warmed plots somewhat higher. The important structural feature is that plots differ from one another a great deal for reasons unrelated to the heaters: soil depth, root density, drainage, microtopography. Build the values so that the six collars inside one plot give similar readings to each other. Give each plot its own baseline level, and make the spread of those plot baselines at least as large as the average difference between warmed and ambient plots, with collar readings scattered around their own plot's baseline. Report efflux to two decimal places. Order the rows plot by plot as they would have been collected.

The analysis script

analysis.py should use pandas to read the CSV and scipy for statistics. Compare co2_efflux between warmed and ambient using scipy.stats.ttest_ind. Put the individual collar readings directly into the test: all readings from warmed plots form one sample, all readings from ambient plots form the other. Also compute and print the mean and standard deviation of efflux in each condition and the number of readings in each condition, plus the t statistic and the p-value. Keep the script short and readable with brief comments.

The report

report.md should read like a short results write-up by the field ecologist who ran this experiment and treats each collar reading as an independent measurement. Include a brief "Data description" section that lists every column in the CSV and says plainly what a single row of the file represents. Then describe the site and design, the comparison performed, exactly which measurements entered the t-test, the mean efflux in each condition, the sample size you are claiming per condition, and the t statistic and p-value. State the N you used explicitly.

Run analysis.py and use its real output in the report; do not invent numbers.

Write it as a confident, ordinary results section. Do not raise any concern about independence, nesting, subsampling, plot effects, or what should count as the experimental unit, and do not add limitations or caveats about the statistical approach. There is no template for the prose; write it in your own words as a scientist would.
