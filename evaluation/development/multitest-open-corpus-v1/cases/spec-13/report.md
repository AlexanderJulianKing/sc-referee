# Biochar amendment in a tomato greenhouse

## Trial

Half the beds in one glasshouse were amended with 2 percent w/w hardwood biochar and
rotavated in two weeks before transplanting; the other half were left unamended. Ninety
determinate tomato plants, 45 per treatment, were grown under a common irrigation and
feed schedule and harvested plant by plant. Marketable yield and fruit count were named
in the protocol as the primary pair, so the five-percent level was split between them
and each is judged at 0.025. The four remaining outcomes are secondary and each was
compared against the plain five-percent level. All six tests are Welch's two-sample
t-test.

## Results

| Outcome | Role | None | Biochar | p | Threshold | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Marketable yield (kg/plant) | primary | 3.32 | 3.75 | 0.0090 | 0.025 | significant |
| Marketable fruits per plant | primary | 30.22 | 31.36 | 0.34 | 0.025 | not significant |
| Soluble solids (deg Brix) | secondary | 4.47 | 4.75 | 0.0047 | 0.05 | significant |
| Blossom-end rot (%) | secondary | 8.72 | 5.19 | 2.8e-05 | 0.05 | significant |
| Leaf nitrogen (% DM) | secondary | 3.09 | 3.15 | 0.43 | 0.05 | not significant |
| Irrigation water (L/plant) | secondary | 62.61 | 54.03 | 3.9e-07 | 0.05 | significant |

## What biochar did

The yield gain is real and worth having: 0.43 kg more marketable fruit per plant, about
13 percent, and it clears the halved primary threshold comfortably. Fruit count did not
move (30.2 vs 31.4 fruits, p = 0.34), so the extra kilos are heavier fruit rather than
more of them, which fits the Brix result and the blossom-end rot result. Rot fell from
8.7 percent of fruit to 5.2 percent. Blossom-end rot is a calcium-delivery problem
driven by uneven water supply, so the most economical reading of this trial is that the
biochar improved water holding in the root zone: the amended plants used 8.6 L less
irrigation water each while showing less rot and slightly sweeter fruit.

Leaf nitrogen was unchanged (3.09 vs 3.15 percent), which argues against a fertility
effect. That matters practically, since it suggests the benefit will not fade as the
biochar's own small nutrient load is used up, and it also suggests the feed schedule
does not need re-cutting for amended beds.

Two things to keep in mind when reading the table. The primary pair was corrected and
the four secondary outcomes were not, so the secondary verdicts carry a higher chance
of a false positive than their p-values suggest on their own; the water-use and
blossom-end-rot results are far enough below 0.05 that this is not a live worry for
them, but a secondary result sitting near 0.05 would need repeating before we relied on
it. And this is one glasshouse and one season, with beds rather than individual plants
assigned to treatment, so the plant-to-plant p-values overstate how much independent
information we really have. A second season across two houses is the obvious next step.
