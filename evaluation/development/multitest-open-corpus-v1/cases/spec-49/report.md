# Replacing half the fish oil with algal oil in salmon smolt diets

Smolts were reared for sixteen weeks in replicate tanks on either a standard marine-oil
diet or a diet in which half the fish oil was replaced with algal oil. 108 fish were
sampled at the end, 54 per diet, and each fish contributes one row. Every outcome was
compared between diets with a two-sample Welch t-test on the difference in means, judged
against the conventional 5 percent cutoff.

## Results

| Outcome | fish_oil | algal_oil | difference | p | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| final weight (g) | 1306 | 1232 | 74 | 0.06056 | not significant |
| specific growth rate (%/day) | 1.203 | 1.133 | 0.071 | 0.01968 | significant |
| fillet EPA+DHA (mg/g) | 12.70 | 16.05 | -3.36 | 2.1e-12 | significant |
| liver lipid (%) | 6.16 | 6.11 | 0.04 | 0.87364 | not significant |
| condition factor | 1.324 | 1.291 | 0.034 | 0.15767 | not significant |
| SalmoFan colour score | 27.2 | 27.7 | -0.6 | 0.13747 | not significant |

## Reading

The fillet fatty acid result is the clear one. Fish on the algal diet carry about 3.4 mg/g
more EPA plus DHA in the fillet, roughly a quarter more than the marine-oil fish, and the
p-value is far from the cutoff. Algal oil is a direct source of DHA, so this is the
expected direction and the size is worth having: it is the outcome that matters most for
the product's nutritional claim.

Growth is the awkward part. Fish on marine oil grew slightly faster, 1.203 against 1.133
percent per day, and that comparison came in at p = 0.020. Final weight moved the same way
by about 74 g, but landed at p = 0.061. So the two growth measures point the same
direction while falling on opposite sides of the cutoff, which is a reminder that neither
should be read as a hard yes or no on its own. A 5 percent difference in growth rate over
sixteen weeks is small in husbandry terms, and a commercial cycle is far longer than this
trial, so the practical question is whether it persists to harvest rather than whether it
crossed 0.05 here.

Liver lipid, condition factor, and colour all came out close between the diets. Nothing in
those three suggests the substitution stresses the fish or costs the fillet its
appearance, and the colour result matters commercially since buyers grade on it.

## Conclusion

Half of the fish oil can be replaced with algal oil without damage to the animals or the
look of the product, and the fillet gains omega-3. The one thing to watch is growth, where
the marine-oil fish held a small edge in both measures. Before this goes to a commercial
site we would want a full production-cycle trial with tank as the unit of replication.
That matters here: these p-values treat individual fish as independent, and fish sharing a
tank are not, so the true precision on the growth comparisons is weaker than these numbers
suggest.
