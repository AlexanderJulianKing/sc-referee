# Night-shift work and metabolic markers

## Survey

One hundred and ten workers at a single distribution centre attended an occupational
health visit: 55 permanent night-shift workers and 55 day-shift workers, matched on age
band and job grade. Fasting bloods and anthropometry were collected on the one
occasion, along with self-reported sleep over 24 hours. Each outcome was compared
between shifts with Welch's two-sample t-test and each p-value judged against the
conventional five-percent cutoff.

## Results

| Outcome | Day | Night | p | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fasting glucose (mmol/L) | 5.12 | 5.33 | 0.041 | significant |
| HDL cholesterol (mmol/L) | 1.39 | 1.24 | 0.011 | significant |
| Triglycerides (mmol/L) | 1.32 | 1.56 | 0.044 | significant |
| Waist circumference (cm) | 92.66 | 96.40 | 0.047 | significant |
| Systolic BP (mmHg) | 123.69 | 127.44 | 0.14 | not significant |
| Sleep per 24 h (hours) | 7.12 | 6.14 | 6.1e-06 | significant |

## What this means for surveillance

The strongest and least ambiguous result is sleep. Night workers reported about an hour
less sleep per 24 hours, and that difference is far clearer than anything else in the
table. The four metabolic differences all point the same way as the shift-work
literature would predict: higher fasting glucose, lower HDL, higher triglycerides, and
a waist about 3.7 cm larger. Blood pressure moved in the same direction but did not
reach the cutoff.

Three of those four metabolic p-values sit between 0.04 and 0.05, which is worth saying
plainly. Individually each clears the cutoff; as a group of six comparisons on the same
110 workers, results clustered that close to the line are the kind that often fail to
reappear in a second sample. The sleep result would survive almost any reasonable
handling of the multiplicity; the glucose, triglyceride, and waist results would not
necessarily. We would treat them as a consistent pattern worth acting on rather than as
four separately established facts.

For surveillance the practical reading is that this combination, larger waist, lower
HDL, higher triglycerides, and higher fasting glucose, is the metabolic syndrome
cluster, so the sensible response is a single periodic check covering all four rather
than four separate triggers. We would suggest annual fasting lipids, glucose or HbA1c,
and waist measurement for permanent night staff, with sleep asked about at the same
visit since it is both the clearest difference here and the one most open to change
through roster design.

Two limits. This is a cross-sectional survey at one site, so nothing here establishes
that night work caused the differences; workers who tolerate nights poorly may already
have left the shift, which would if anything make the night group look healthier than
the exposure warrants. And sleep is self-reported at a visit where the purpose of the
survey was known.
