# Silvopasture versus open pasture for sheep

One summer on one farm, 68 ewe-lamb pairs split evenly between a poplar-alley
silvopasture system and adjacent open pasture. Six outcomes were compared with Welch
two-sample t-tests.

The three animal-performance outcomes (lamb gain, ewe condition change, panting score)
were the primary group and were corrected together with a Bonferroni threshold of
0.05 / 3 = 0.0167. The three system outcomes were read at 0.05.

## Results

| Outcome | Open | Silvopasture | p | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lamb average daily gain (g/d) | 238.6 | 261.4 | 0.048 | not significant |
| Ewe body condition change | -0.32 | -0.13 | 0.0028 | significant |
| Midday panting score | 2.07 | 1.31 | 4.6e-07 | significant |
| Faecal egg count (epg) | 481.6 | 613.2 | 0.016 | significant |
| Pasture growth (kg DM/ha/d) | 42.0 | 34.1 | 0.0036 | significant |
| Wool clip per ewe (kg) | 3.17 | 3.22 | 0.69 | not significant |

## Interpretation

The clearest result is heat load. Midday panting scores fell from 2.07 to 1.31 under the
poplar alleys, a difference so large relative to the spread that no plausible threshold
would change the call. Ewes in silvopasture also lost less condition over the summer,
-0.13 against -0.32 of a body condition score, which is what you expect if animals spend
less of the day standing and panting and more of it grazing.

Lamb growth points the same way, 22.8 g/day faster in silvopasture, but at p = 0.048 it
does not clear the corrected threshold for the animal-performance group and is reported
as not significant. With 34 pairs per system this study cannot resolve a gain of that
size. It is worth noting that the lamb result and the ewe condition result are two views
of the same shade mechanism, so the supported condition finding lends the growth
difference some plausibility even though the growth test on its own does not stand.

The system outcomes describe the cost side. Pasture growth was about 8 kg DM/ha/day
lower under the trees, roughly a fifth less feed grown per day, which is the direct
price of the poplar canopy and would eventually show up as reduced stocking rate. Worm
burdens were higher in silvopasture, 613 against 482 eggs per gram. Cooler, shadier,
moister swards favour larval survival, so this is a mechanism to plan for rather than a
surprise, and it points at a heavier reliance on faecal egg count monitoring and
targeted drenching in a silvopasture system. Wool clip did not differ.

The case for silvopasture on this farm is a heat-stress case. In a summer where open
pasture ewes were panting at midday, the alleys bought measurable welfare and condition
benefits, plus the timber and shelter value not measured here, at the cost of about a
fifth of pasture production and a worse worm picture. That trade looks worthwhile on
farms with hot summers and worse on farms where feed supply is already the binding
constraint. One summer on one farm is a thin basis for either conclusion, and the
readings should be repeated across seasons before the system is rolled out.
