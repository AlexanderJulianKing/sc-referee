# Green roofs and building microclimate

Fifty flat-roofed municipal buildings were instrumented for one summer, 25 of them
retrofitted with extensive green roofs and 25 left as conventional membrane roofs. Each
building contributes one row of season summaries. Every outcome was compared between the
two roof types with a Welch two-sample t-test at the five percent level.

## Results

| Outcome | Conventional | Green | p | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Peak roof surface temp (C) | 58.50 | 41.78 | 1.3e-12 | significant difference |
| Summer cooling energy (kWh/m2) | 33.94 | 29.96 | 0.073 | no significant difference |
| Daily indoor temp swing (C) | 4.42 | 3.63 | 0.0036 | significant difference |
| Stormwater runoff coefficient | 0.87 | 0.51 | 1.1e-13 | significant difference |
| Annual maintenance (hours) | 12.92 | 21.33 | 1.9e-06 | significant difference |

## Interpretation

The physical effects on the roof itself are large and easy to see. Green roof surfaces
peaked about 16.7 C cooler than membrane roofs, and the runoff coefficient fell from
0.87 to 0.51, meaning roughly a third less of each storm leaves the roof as runoff. Both
differences are far larger than the building-to-building spread, so neither depends on
how the test is set up.

Indoor conditions follow, but more weakly. The daily indoor temperature swing was 0.8 C
smaller under green roofs, which matters for occupant comfort in buildings without
active cooling. Metered cooling energy was 4.0 kWh/m2 lower under green roofs, about 12
percent, but with a p-value of 0.073 the summer does not establish that difference. The
honest statement is that the energy saving is plausible and consistent with the
temperature results, and that this study is too small to confirm it. Fifty buildings
with a between-building SD near 7.5 kWh/m2 leave little power for an effect of this size.

Maintenance runs the other way and is well established: green roofs took about 8.4 more
hours per year, roughly 70 percent more upkeep. That is a real recurring cost, not a
rounding error, and it must sit in the business case next to the benefits.

For the municipal estate the case rests mainly on stormwater. Halving the runoff
coefficient across a portfolio of flat roofs is worth real money wherever drainage
capacity is the binding constraint, and the roof temperature drop should extend membrane
life. Energy savings should be treated as an unproven bonus rather than a line item
until a larger or longer study is done. Buildings whose occupants suffer from summer
overheating and buildings on drainage-constrained catchments are the ones to retrofit
first.
