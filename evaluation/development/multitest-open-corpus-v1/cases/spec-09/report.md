# Reduced-salt processed cheese spread

The spread was reformulated to cut sodium by about a third using a potassium chloride
blend, and compared against the standard formulation over a single production run. Sixty
200 g packs were drawn across the run, 30 per formulation, and each pack is an independent
sample. Six outcomes were measured: sodium content, texture-analyser firmness, melt spread
diameter, pH, and trained-panel scores for saltiness and bitterness. Each outcome went
through the same testing routine, a Welch two-sample t-test judged at the usual 5% cutoff.

| Outcome | Standard | Reduced salt | p | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium (mg/100 g) | 1112.0 | 741.6 | <0.0001 | difference between formulations |
| Firmness (N) | 2.88 | 2.64 | 0.0091 | difference between formulations |
| Meltability (mm) | 48.3 | 51.1 | 0.0092 | difference between formulations |
| pH | 5.71 | 5.67 | 0.1329 | no difference detected |
| Panel saltiness (0-10) | 6.52 | 5.40 | <0.0001 | difference between formulations |
| Panel bitterness (0-10) | 1.13 | 2.41 | <0.0001 | difference between formulations |

The reformulation hit its target: sodium fell by 370 mg per 100 g, a 33% cut, which is
what the brief asked for. pH is unchanged, so the acid balance and the shelf-life
assumptions that rest on it carry over from the standard product.

The cost shows up in texture and in flavour. The reduced-salt packs are softer by 0.24 N
and spread about 2.8 mm further on melt, both differences that a production line and a
consumer can notice, though neither is large enough on its own to put the product outside
the category. Saltiness dropped by 1.1 points on the panel scale, which is expected when a
third of the sodium goes, and the potassium chloride brought bitterness up from 1.13 to
2.41, more than doubling it.

Recommendation: the reduced-salt version is acceptable on composition, pH and melt
behaviour, and the softening is within what the process can be tuned to absorb. The
bitterness rise is the one result that should hold up a launch. It is the largest sensory
change in the set and it moves in the direction consumers reject first. Before scaling, I
would run a bitterness-masking trial, for example with a yeast extract or a lower
potassium fraction made up with another mineral salt, and repeat the panel work with
consumers rather than trained assessors, since a trained panel is tuned to detect
bitterness that a shopper may not object to. Nothing here argues for abandoning the
reformulation.
