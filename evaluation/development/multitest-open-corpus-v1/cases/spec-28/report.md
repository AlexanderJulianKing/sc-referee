# Fluoride varnish in a paediatric dental programme

Nurseries were allocated to twice-yearly fluoride varnish or to oral health advice
alone. After two years, 150 children were examined by calibrated dentists, 75 per
programme. Four outcomes were compared with Welch two-sample t-tests.

## Multiplicity

The four outcomes are reported as one family, so the five percent error rate is spent
across the family rather than on each test:

    0.05 / 4 = 0.0125

Each p-value is judged against 0.0125. Nothing is called significant at 0.05.

## Results

| Outcome | Advice only | Varnish | p | Against 0.0125 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| dmft count | 3.31 | 2.51 | 0.017 | not supported |
| New carious lesions (2 years) | 1.68 | 1.12 | 0.0044 | supported |
| Teeth extracted for caries | 0.45 | 0.20 | 0.017 | not supported |
| Plaque index | 1.42 | 1.40 | 0.75 | not supported |

## Interpretation

One effect of the varnish programme is supported. Children in varnish nurseries
developed about 0.56 fewer new carious lesions over the two years, a third fewer than
under advice alone, and that difference clears the corrected threshold with room to
spare. New lesions are also the outcome the programme should move most directly, since
varnish acts on surfaces that are still sound at the time of application.

Two further results point the same way but do not clear the threshold. The dmft count
was 0.80 teeth lower and extractions were 0.25 per child lower under varnish, both with
p near 0.017. At the uncorrected five percent level both would have been called
significant, and that is exactly the situation the correction exists for: with four
tests, a p-value of 0.017 is not surprising under no effect. These two should be read as
consistent with the supported lesion result rather than as separate findings. dmft in
particular carries the history of decay from before the programme started, so it is a
blunter instrument than new lesions.

Plaque index is flat, 1.42 against 1.40. That is expected. Fluoride varnish is not a
cleaning intervention, and neither arm's oral hygiene appears to have shifted, which
also suggests the lesion result is not explained by varnish families brushing more.

For service planning, the defensible claim is that twice-yearly varnish reduces new
caries in this population. The tooth-level and extraction savings that would drive the
cost case are suggested but not established at 150 children. A larger sample, or
follow-up of this cohort into the permanent dentition, would settle whether the lesion
reduction translates into fewer extractions.
